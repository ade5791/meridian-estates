Meridian Estates

An original 3D property-trading board game. Buy streets, build up blocks, charge rent, and squeeze your rivals into bankruptcy. Runs in the browser - nothing to install.

Play Meridian Estates

How to play

  • Goal. Be the last player who is not bankrupt. If nobody is bankrupted before the round cap, the highest net worth wins.
  • Game length. Choose it on the setup screen: Short (30 rounds), Standard (60, the default), Long (120) or Marathon (400). The current round and the cap are shown in the header at all times, so the finish line is always visible.
  • Speed. The Speed button in the utility bar cycles 4x / 8x / 1x / 2x (the game ships at 4x) and is available at any time, including while an AI opponent is taking its turn. It scales animation length and AI thinking time only - the dice and card order are driven by the seed, so speed never changes the outcome of a game.
  • Turn. Roll two dice, move that many tiles, then resolve the tile you land on. Rolling doubles gives another roll; three doubles in a row sends you to the Holding House.
  • Buying. Land on an unowned street, rail or utility and you may buy it at the listed price. Decline and it goes to a sealed-bid auction open to every solvent player.
  • Rent. Land on someone else's property and you pay rent. Owning a whole colour group doubles the base rent on undeveloped streets. Rails and utilities scale with how many the owner holds. Mortgaged property charges no rent.
  • Building. Own a full colour group, then buy houses - up to four, then a hotel. Houses must be built evenly across the group; the Build button is disabled when the even-build rule would reject the purchase.
  • Mortgage. Raise cash against a property. Unmortgaging costs the mortgage value plus 10% interest.
  • Holding House (jail). Pay the fine, spend a Writ of Release card, or roll doubles. After three failed turns you pay the fine and move anyway.
  • Cards. Two original decks - Fortune and Ledger - move you, pay you, charge you, or hand you a Writ of Release.
  • Trading. Offer any mix of properties and cash to another player at any point on your turn after rolling.
  • Bankruptcy. Owe more than you can raise and you are out; your assets pass to your creditor, or back to the bank.

Setup and review states

One to four seats, each independently human or AI. The game is deterministic: the same seed replays the same dice and the same card order.

Runtime resilience

  • Fixed light pool. Scene light count sampled every 500 ms for a complete 35-round AI game with all effects firing. Distinct values observed: [8]. The light count never changes at runtime.
  • Black-frame watchdog. Two forced black reads make the plain forward renderer permanent; the game stays playable.
  • Frame-error containment. Five injected frame errors drop post-processing and the game keeps advancing.
  • Leak audit. Five in-page restarts plus 30 s idle return exactly to the warm baseline: canvases 1 to 1, DOM nodes 103 to 103, geometries 78 to 78, textures 51 to 51, shader programs 6 to 6, lights 8 to 8.

Mobile

  • Every touch target measures at least 44x44 px.
  • The primary action sits in the lower, one-hand-reachable half of a portrait screen.
  • Rotating portrait to landscape mid-game leaves the serialized game state byte-identical.
  • No horizontal overflow; safe-area insets respected.
Not verified. These are the honest limits of what was measured:
  • No testing on real physical phones or tablets - all touch results come from browser touch emulation.
  • No screen-reader or full accessibility audit.
  • The frame-time matrix was captured on one GPU (RTX 3090) in headless Chromium. Other GPUs, integrated graphics and mobile SoCs are not characterised.
  • No multi-device or networked multiplayer - play is hotseat plus AI on one machine.
  • Visual quality has not been judged against any external reference.

Technical notes

  • Three.js r180, ES modules, no build step and no framework.
  • Zero external runtime dependencies - Three.js is served from this same origin, so the game keeps working if any CDN is unreachable.
  • The rules engine is headless: it has no reference to the DOM or to Three.js, and is covered directly by unit tests.
  • Subsystems never import one another; they communicate through a documented event vocabulary that a conformance test diffs against ARCHITECTURE.md on every run.
  • Dice and both card decks draw from named, seeded, serializable random streams, so a save reloads into a byte-identical future.
  • Audio is generated procedurally with the Web Audio API - no audio files are shipped.
